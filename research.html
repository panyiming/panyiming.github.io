Lendable Inventory Concentration, Recall Risk, and Equity Pricing Anomalies (sole-authored)

SSRN

A short seller needs borrowed shares until the mispricing corrects, but lenders can recall them at any time. When lendable inventory concentration (IC) is high, meaning that a few large lenders hold most of the lendable shares, one lender's withdrawal can recall more shares than borrowers can replace in time. Within firms, a one-standard-deviation higher IC predicts a 2.1 percentage point higher next-month recall probability (mean 10.9%), controlling for the fee and lendable supply. This recall risk deters short selling: the same overpricing signal implied by anomalies attracts about one quarter as much short interest at the highest IC as at the lowest IC, although unused shares are available. As a result, less of the overpricing is corrected: monthly anomaly spreads are 0.43% larger in the top than in the bottom IC quintile, mainly through the short leg, and remain 0.33% larger net of fees. Russell 3000 inclusion, which broadens the lender base, lowers recall risk and raises short interest most in overpriced stocks with high pre-inclusion IC. Beyond the fee, inventory concentration makes access to lendable shares less durable, which deters short selling and sustains anomaly-related overpricing.

Key result. Anomaly returns are concentrated in stocks whose lendable inventory is held by a few lenders (high IC). For each stock, NetLeg is the number of anomalies that place it in the long leg minus the number that place it in the short leg. Within each IC group, I sort stocks into NetLeg quintiles and go long Q5 (most underpriced) and short Q1 (most overpriced).

Short-Sale Constraints and the Return Predictability of Idiosyncratic Variance: Evidence from Crypto Perpetuals (with Jia Zhai)

High idiosyncratic variance predicts low future returns in cryptocurrency markets, and the relation comes from large, recent upside price shocks in coins that cannot be shorted. To study this relation, we use the staggered expansion of access to short selling across coins and compare the same coin's returns after shocks before and after it can be shorted. After upside shocks, prices reverse over the following week, and access to short selling reduces this reversal by 2.55 percentage points, about three quarters of its size. After downside shocks, by contrast, access matters mainly when the decline follows an upside shock. Consistent with these results, access also weakens the return predictability of recent upside shocks, by a similar amount. Idiosyncratic variance thus predicts lower returns in part because, when pessimistic investors cannot sell short, prices rise above the level implied by the average belief after a shock and fall back as disagreement fades.
