Fee or Quantity? Lender Base Concentration and the Effective Short-sale Constraint

Job Market Paper

Borrowing fees measure the spot price of borrowing shares, but short arbitrage requires durable borrow access. I study this non-price dimension of short-sale constraints through the concentration of lendable inventory across lenders. Using securities-lending data, I show that concentrated lendable supply predicts greater borrow fragility: quantity responds less elastically to borrowing demand, while fees become more sensitive to utilization and more unstable. High-concentration stocks also incorporate negative information more slowly and exhibit substantially larger anomaly returns, with the difference concentrated in the short leg. The evidence suggests that lender concentration is a structural source of effective short-sale constraints beyond observed borrowing fees.

Prediction Targets for Cross-Sectional Bond Selection

Machine-learning models are typically trained to forecast returns but used to rank securities into long–short portfolios. I show that this mismatch matters in corporate bonds. A return forecast partly fits market-wide return variation that is common across bonds and therefore uninformative for cross-sectional selection. Holding the information set and model architecture fixed, I instead forecast the probability that a bond outperforms the contemporaneous cross-sectional median. Probability forecasts produce substantially stronger cross-sectional rankings and long–short portfolios with lower systematic risk. The results show that the prediction target itself is an economically important design choice in machine-learning asset selection.

Shortability and Price Discovery in Cryptocurrency Markets

When investors cannot sell short, theory predicts that prices can overreact to positive information and underreact to negative information. I test these predictions using the staggered introduction of perpetual futures across cryptocurrencies. Prior to becoming shortable, large positive price jumps are followed by substantial reversal, whereas negative jumps are followed by continued declines. Both asymmetries decline sharply after perpetual futures listing. The results provide asset-level evidence that short-selling access improves the incorporation of both positive and negative information into prices.
